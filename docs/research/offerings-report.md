# Sell Proven Patterns, Not AI Pioneering

SureGround should add five offerings beyond its three core services: a security-first **AI Safe Start** review (shadow-AI inventory, vendor risk checks, acceptable-use policy), a fixed-fee **AI Opportunity Assessment**, an **AI Pilot Triage** sprint for stalled projects, a **Security Questionnaire and RFP Answer Engine** for B2B sellers, and a **Catalog and Feed Readiness** sprint for eCommerce brands. The core engineering and agent work should then be sold as a fixed-scope "proven pattern" sprint, followed by two retainers: managed AI operations and a hands-on fractional AI solutions engineer. Published US benchmarks put boutique readiness audits at **$3,500 to $8,500**, single-workflow or knowledge-base builds at **$10,000 to $45,000**, managed support at **$500 to $2,500 per month**, and fractional AI leadership at **$3,500 to $20,000 per month**. The demand data supports a cautious ladder rather than a big first ask. Federal and bank-transaction data put regular paid business use of AI at only **17 to 20%**, owner surveys put casual use at 55 to 77%, and just **14%** of owners say AI is embedded in core operations. The barriers that stop buyers are trust problems: **data privacy (33%)** and **distrust of accuracy (31%)** lead, and cost ranks lower. Tennessee ranks **47th of 50** in small business AI use at 46%. A Nashville buyer who feels like a pioneer is in fact behind, and the pitch should be "catch up safely with a pattern that has worked elsewhere." David's record answering CISO security reviews, running a 500-question RFP with custom tooling, transforming product feeds at Feedonomics, and converting POCs into production deals maps directly onto the trust, data, and revenue problems that generic AI strategists and productized freelancers leave open.

## Only 14% of small businesses have AI running real operations

The demand picture depends on how adoption is measured, and the gap between measures is where the work is. The US Census Bureau's Business Trends and Outlook Survey held business AI use between **17% and 20%** from December 2025 through May 2026, reaching 19.8% in early May ([Census Bureau](https://census.gov/library/stories/2026/05/ai-use-businesses.html)). The JPMorganChase Institute, using transaction data from more than 4.6 million small businesses, found that **17.7%** paid for any AI service in December 2025, and that the median adopter spends only about **$28 to $30 per month**, roughly the cost of one chat subscription ([JPMorganChase Institute](https://www.jpmorganchase.com/institute/all-topics/business-growth-and-entrepreneurship/understanding-ai-use-by-small-businesses)). Owner surveys report much higher numbers. Goldman Sachs found 76% of its small business cohort using AI in early 2026, but only **14% said it was fully embedded in core operations** ([Goldman Sachs](https://www.goldmansachs.com/pressroom/press-releases/2026/small-businesses-embrace-ai-but-need-training-and-support-to-fully-harness-it)). Intuit found that 77% of US owners use AI regularly but only 12% paid for dedicated AI tools ([Intuit](https://www.intuit.com/blog/global-stories/ai-impact-report/)). Most SMBs are dabblers who prompt a general-purpose chatbot. They do not need persuading that AI exists. They need someone to wire it into how the business runs.

Firm size defines the buyer. From December 2025 to May 2026, Census found AI use rising only among firms with **20 or more employees**, with no significant change among smaller firms. It reached 32% at firms with 100 to 249 employees and 37% at firms with 250 or more ([Census Bureau](https://census.gov/library/stories/2026/05/ai-use-businesses.html)). Budgets follow the same split. A Paychex survey of firms under 500 employees found **51% spend $1,000 to $9,999 per year on AI** in total, including subscriptions ([Paychex](https://www.paychex.com/articles/human-resources/small-business-ai-impact)). Among firms with 10 or more employees, Tech.co found that more than two-thirds spend $10,000 or more and over 20% spend $50,000 to $100,000 ([Tech.co](https://tech.co/news/businesses-spend-50k-100k-ai)). SureGround's sweet spot is roughly 10 to 250 employees. Below that, an entry offer must fit inside a sub-$10,000 annual AI budget, and a $10,000 to $30,000 build needs a firm with real process complexity. By sector, professional services (30.3%) and information firms (39.3%) adopt most, and construction (8.9%) and transportation (5.4%) adopt least ([JPMorganChase Institute](https://www.jpmorganchase.com/institute/all-topics/business-growth-and-entrepreneurship/understanding-ai-use-by-small-businesses)).

Two signals show where integration work is already happening without supervision. A Pax8 survey of 1,150 SMB leaders found that more than half have built automated workflows connecting AI to business systems, two-thirds feel they must adopt AI within six months, and only **36% formally review or approve the AI tools built internally** ([Channel Dive](https://www.channeldive.com/news/smbs-lack-ai-governance-report/832804/)). More than a third found unapproved AI use in the past six months ([Channel Dive](https://www.channeldive.com/news/smbs-lack-ai-governance-report/832804/)). SMBs are building ad hoc integrations that nobody has secured, tested, or documented, and that is a cleanup and governance market as much as a build market. On use cases, Bluevine's 2026 survey ranks data analysis (39%), marketing and sales (37%), operations automation (29%), and customer service (27%) as current uses ([Bluevine via KRDO](https://krdo.com/stacker-small-business/2026/07/16/3-4-of-small-businesses-dont-trust-ai-for-basic-tasks/)). MIT's enterprise research found that more than half of generative AI budgets go to sales and marketing tools, while the **largest ROI came from back-office automation** that cut outsourcing and agency costs ([Fortune](https://fortune.com/2025/08/18/mit-report-95-percent-generative-ai-pilots-at-companies-failing-cfo/?CMP=techscape_email)). Buyers spend on the visible front office, but the money is in the back office, and that mismatch is an opening for a consultant who can measure savings.

## Nervous buyers fear leaks and wrong answers, not price

The barrier data contradicts the assumption that SMBs stall on cost. Bluevine's April 2026 survey of 942 owners found that 82% face at least one barrier. **Data security and privacy led at 33%, up from 23% a year earlier**, followed by distrust of AI accuracy at 31%, tool cost at 24%, and "not seeing enough value yet" at 20%. **78% do not fully trust AI to handle even low-level tasks without oversight** ([Bluevine via KRDO](https://krdo.com/stacker-small-business/2026/07/16/3-4-of-small-businesses-dont-trust-ai-for-basic-tasks/)). Among North Carolina businesses not planning to use AI, the Census cut found about 63% saying it was not applicable to their operations, 22% citing lack of knowledge, 22% citing privacy, and only about **7% citing cost** ([NC Commerce](https://www.commerce.nc.gov/news/the-lead-feed/data-show-limited-ai-adoption-among-north-carolina-businesses)). Intuit states plainly that cost is not the biggest barrier and that owners worry about AI becoming "just another tool to manage" ([Intuit](https://www.intuit.com/blog/global-stories/ai-impact-report/)). Two barriers matter most for offer design. "Not applicable" is a discovery problem, which a paid assessment that maps the buyer's own workflows solves. Privacy and accuracy distrust are a trust problem, which an offer built around guardrails, approved tools, and human review solves. When buyers object to price, the real objection is usually unclear ROI, so the answer is a measured business case rather than a discount.

Owners also say they need help to make progress. **73% of Goldman's cohort say more training and implementation resources would help**, and their top barriers are lack of technical expertise, difficulty choosing among tools, and data privacy ([Goldman Sachs](https://www.goldmansachs.com/pressroom/press-releases/2026/small-businesses-embrace-ai-but-need-training-and-support-to-fully-harness-it)). Thryv's 2026 survey found 70% need more training and 57% learn mainly from YouTube and social media ([CPA Practice Advisor](https://www.cpapracticeadvisor.com/?p=186565)). Generic AI literacy, however, is turning into a free public good. The US Chamber and its foundation now offer free AI training courses for small businesses nationwide ([US Chamber](https://www.uschamber.com/small-business/u-s-chamber-of-commerce-and-u-s-chamber-foundation-launch-free-ai-training-courses-for-small-businesses-nationwide)). SureGround's core enablement offer should therefore be specific to each business, built on the client's own documents and tools, and should not compete with free webinars.

The "pioneer" fear does not match the facts, and that is the most useful finding for messaging. No high-quality survey isolates fear of being an early adopter as a named barrier, so this framing rests on proxies. Salesforce found that 80% of AI users think their peers commonly use AI, while only about a third of non-users think so. **Non-users systematically underestimate how far their peers have moved** ([Salesforce](https://salesforce.com/news/stories/smbs-ai-trends-2025)). The US Chamber found **80% of owners increased their AI plans after learning competitors were using it** ([IPWatchdog](https://ipwatchdog.com/2025/08/18/us-chamber-report-small-businesses-rapidly-adopting-ai-despite-regulatory-concerns/)). Competitive pressure moves these buyers more than novelty does. Locally, US Chamber data puts **Tennessee small business AI use at 46%, 47th of 50 states**, against a national figure of 58% ([AOL/Benzinga](https://www.aol.com/articles/top-states-using-ai-small-193209883.html)). A Nashville owner is not going first. They are behind peers in most other states, and the low-risk move is to adopt patterns those peers have already proven.

Pilot failure data supports the same message, with one caveat. MIT NANDA's 2025 study found about **95% of enterprise generative AI pilots produced no measurable P&L impact**, while purchases from specialized vendors and partnerships succeeded **about 67% of the time**, versus about one-third as often for internal builds ([Fortune](https://fortune.com/2025/08/18/mit-report-95-percent-generative-ai-pilots-at-companies-failing-cfo/?CMP=techscape_email)). Mid-market firms moved from pilot to full implementation in about **90 days**, against nine months or more for large enterprises ([Virtualization Review](https://virtualizationreview.com/articles/2025/08/19/mit-report-finds-most-ai-business-investments-fail-reveals-genai-divide.aspx)). The study is enterprise-weighted, small-sample, and defines success narrowly, so SureGround should not quote it as an SMB failure rate. It still justifies a fixed 90-day production milestone and a "partner, don't go it alone" posture. That finding creates a tension for an engineering firm, because MIT's data favors buying over building. The honest resolution is that SureGround configures and integrates proven tools the client buys, and writes custom code only for the thin layer that connects those tools to the client's data. Clients get the partnered path that succeeds, not a science project.

Two more barriers shape scope. Gartner found that half of consumers prefer brands that avoid generative AI ([Fortune](https://fortune.com/2026/03/18/small-business-ai-slow-integration-across-operations/)), so first projects should sit in the back office, where a misfire does not reach customers. The US Chamber found **65% of owners worry about state-law compliance** and only 31% feel prepared for proposed AI disclosure and oversight rules ([IPWatchdog](https://ipwatchdog.com/2025/08/18/us-chamber-report-small-businesses-rapidly-adopting-ai-despite-regulatory-concerns/)). That anxiety gives a governance offer a natural reason to recur.

These are SureGround's strongest fits, so SureGround will set its own anchor in those categories rather than follow one. Hourly rates for independent US AI consultants cluster at **$150 to $350**, with senior strategy work at $250 to $500 ([Ryan Doser](https://ryandoser.com/ai-consultant-cost/)). That last point is SureGround's opening: a **fractional AI solutions engineer** who both advises and ships. Sales engineering demo enablement and customer-facing AI features for SaaS are areas where David's fit is outstanding, but the research found no SMB demand data for them. They belong on the website as case studies, such as the FullStory StoryAI session summaries, rather than as priced offers.

## Risk reversal belongs inside the offer, not the sales call

The ladder has to do three jobs for a nervous buyer: make the first purchase small, make every later purchase cheaper to say yes to, and make the vendor carry the delivery risk. The first paid rung should sit **under $5,000**, so it fits inside the $1,000 to $9,999 annual AI budget that half of small firms report ([Paychex](https://www.paychex.com/articles/human-resources/small-business-ai-impact)). Every diagnostic fee should credit toward the next rung within 60 days, following Petronella and Main & Machine. The build sprint should carry a written fixed price, milestone payments, and a "live in 90 days or we keep working at no charge" promise. The 90-day window matches MIT's mid-market pilot-to-production benchmark, which makes the promise both credible and achievable. Retainers should be month-to-month after a short minimum, with model and hosting costs either bundled or itemized up front, because buyers expect hidden costs of 20 to 40% on top of consulting fees ([Ryan Doser](https://ryandoser.com/ai-consultant-cost/)).

Delivery defaults matter as much as terms. Because 78% of owners will not trust AI without oversight, every build should ship with human-approval points and a written data-handling note. First projects should be back-office work, away from skeptical customers. Each build should also capture the client's own documents, SOPs, and history, since MIT traced pilot failure to tools that never learn the business's context ([Virtualization Review](https://virtualizationreview.com/articles/2025/08/19/mit-report-finds-most-ai-business-investments-fail-reveals-genai-divide.aspx)). Vendor surveys report that AI users save 20 or more hours and $500 to $2,000 per month ([Thryv](https://investor.thryv.com/news/news-details/2025/AI-Adoption-Among-Small-Businesses-Surges-41-in-2025-According-to-New-Survey-from-Thryv/)). Those figures can frame expectations if attributed, but they are self-reported, so SureGround should measure baseline hours in the assessment and report against its own numbers.

## Conclusion

The real competitor is not other consultants. It is the $28-a-month chat subscription that lets an owner feel the AI box is checked. SureGround wins by turning that dabbling into embedded, supervised systems, and the way in is trust, because trust is the binding constraint in every neutral survey. That inverts the usual consultancy pitch. David's most saleable asset is not prompt engineering or agent frameworks, which are commoditizing. It is years spent on the other side of the table from CISOs and procurement teams, knowing which answers survive a security review. The market gaps reinforce this: governance, questionnaire, and feed-readiness services have no published SMB price anchors, which gives SureGround room to set prices in exactly the categories where it is most credible.

Recurring revenue is the second implication. In the Consulting Success study, 41% of consultants wanted retainer clients but had none ([Consulting Success](https://www.consultingsuccess.com/consulting-fees-study)), and the published ladders that work all route each project into a monthly relationship. For a solo firm the ladder's economics depend on the managed and fractional rungs, so every diagnostic and sprint should end with a written proposal for one of them. The prices below sit at the low end of boutique ranges and above the productized tier.
